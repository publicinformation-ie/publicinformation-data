### 1 Executive Summary

*Pages 3–5*

This Progress Report on the Climate Action Plan (CAP) 2025 details progress on thenew

actions that were due for completion in Q2 and Q3 2025. In total, 23 of the 39 new Q2

and Q3 actions have been delivered on time (Table 1).

Progress on Actions from Q1 has been made, with 5 of the 6 Q1 actions now delivered

to date (Table 2).

High impact actions delayed from CAP24 and CAP23 are also included in this report. Of

the 54 legacy actions remaining, 10were completed in Q2 and Q3 2025 (Table 3).

Delivery results in Q2 and Q3 show successful implementation has been achieved in

many cases, but difficulties remain in delivering certain actions on time. Accelerating the

implementation of actions will be required, if Ireland is to close the emissions gap.

Delays in climate action delivery over this period are attributed to several factors

including: time required for legislative responses; delays arising from public consultation;

administrative hold-ups such as approval processes, technical complexity or data gaps;

staff resourcing problems; dependencies on other actions or policies; problems

resourcing areas requiring specific technical expertise, and the need for additional

analysis. These delays threaten Ireland’s ability to meet binding emissions targets and

risk increasing future pressures, costs, and reputational harm.

All delayed actions will continue to be reported upon within the CAP25 reporting

process, maintaining visibility as to their delivery alongside new measures.

To provide more detail on risks and delivery profiles, this report is organised into sectoral

chapters in order of emissions share, with a further chapter on cross-cutting activity.As

in the Q1 report, attention is given to Key Performance Indicators (KPIs), national

emissions trends and projections, and high impact action case studies throughout this

report.

Information on each reportable action from the CAP25 Annex of Actions is contained at

Appendix 1.

### 1.1 Climate change news and activity in Q2 and Q3 2025

*Pages 5–7*

In April, the Government formally approved the Climate Action Plan 2025 which details the

climate policies and actions required for Ireland to reduce greenhouse gas emissions. The plan

emphasises accelerating renewable energy, scaling up retrofits, expanding low-emission

transport, and improving adaptation planning.

The urgent need to accelerate Ireland’s climate response was reinforced by a number of reports

released over the past two quarters. The EPA’s greenhouse gas projections from May indicated

that if all current measures are fully implemented, Ireland is on course to achieve only a 9 - 23%

reduction by 2030 and fall short of its statutory 51% target. The first carbon budget (2021–

2025) is now projected to be exceeded by 8–12 Mt CO₂eq.

The EPA’s first National Climate Change Risk Assessment identified 115 climate-related risks

with urgent vulnerabilities highlighted in energy, communications, transport, and coastal systems.

The recent Annual Review on Preparing for Ireland’s Changing Climate from the Climate Change

Advisory Council (CCAC) emphasised the urgency of implementing measures to ensure Ireland's

resilience against climate change. While the European Environment Agency’s State of Europe's

Environment 2025 report noted Ireland’s reductions in carbon emissions and improvements in

energy and transport, it also highlighted the need for further efforts in these areas to achieve a

sustainable economy. These findings underline the need for much faster implementation of

actions already set out in the Climate Action Plan.

Globally, the climate crisis was underscored by new figures from the Internal Displacement

Monitoring Centre (IDMC) showing that war and natural disasters displaced a record 83.4 million

people worldwide last year—equivalent to the entire population of Germany.

Energy

In April, the CCACs Electricity Sectoral Review highlighted that storms, including Darragh and

Éowyn, exposed vulnerabilities in electricity infrastructure and emphasised the risks affecting

water, telecoms, transport, health and agriculture. It warned that, while electricity emissions fell

by about 7% from 2023 to 2024, the current pace of renewable deployment and investment in

grid infrastructure remains insufficient; some renewable capacity already in use cannot be fully

utilised due to grid constraints. Key CCAC recommendations include accelerating grid resilience,

improving planning permissions for existing wind projects, and enhancing demand flexibility

measures.

Eirgrid's Winter Outlook forecast for this year is encouraging, as it indicates a low risk for power

shortages. This positive outlook is due to a number of factors, including the completion of the

new Greenlink Interconnector in April, which represents a significant advancement in Ireland's

energy transition and security. In September, ESB announced that it had successfully installed

over two million smart meters, with four out of five households now having a smart meter. This

roll-out aims to promote efficient energy use, reduce consumption, and support climate targets

and the clean energy transition.

Offshore Wind Development

In April, EirGrid launched a €1 billion multi-year procurement programme for Ireland’s offshore

electricity grid. This programme, covering offshore substations and associated

subsea/underground cables, will be a key enabler for connecting future offshore wind capacity

to the grid.

Ireland’s offshore wind potential came into focus with a report for InterTradeIreland (ITI) by

consultants Everoze, highlighting opportunities for a globally competitive supply chain if

developed on an all-island basis.

Ireland has one of the EU’s most ambitious expansion targets, aiming to deliver 5GW of offshore

wind by 2030, with potential to create tens of thousands of jobs and drive regional investment.

In September, the government published a proposal for the National Designated Maritime Area

Plan (DMAP) for Offshore Renewable Energy (ORE). This plan aims to designate locations

throughout Ireland's entire marine area that have the potential to generate at least 15 GW of

offshore wind energy by 2040. This initiative will support the national target of reaching 20 GW

of offshore wind capacity by the same year.

In September, the Taoiseach along with Minister O’Brien established an Offshore Wind Energy

Clearing House to address identified impediments to the timely delivery of a vibrant offshore

renewable energy sector. Compromising of Secretaries General from relevant Government as

well as industry representatives, it supports and reinforces the work of the Offshore Wind

Delivery Taskforce, especially where issues cannot be resolved at Taskforce level and require an

additional high-level intervention from the centre of Government.

Investment

Green investment momentum continued, with the Ireland Strategic Investment Fund (ISIF)

pledging over €160 million to climate technology businesses. Operating under the NTMA, ISIF

has earmarked €1 billion for a second climate action fund to be allocated over the next four

years, targeting emissions reduction, renewable energy, and carbon capture.

| The Renewable Electricity Spatial Policy Framework aims to translate renewable electricity objectives, as those outlined in Climate Action Plan 2023, to the regional level and allocate regional spatial and renewable generation capacity targets. This will then form the basis under which specific targets at local authority level can be established and subsequently planned for. These targets are key to achieving Ireland’s national renewable electricity targets and will fast-track the roll-out and delivery of grid-scale onshore wind and solar development. The Regional Renewable Electricity Capacity Allocations are to be incorporated into Regional Renewable Energy Strategies (forming part of the wider Regional Spatial and Economic Strategies), which will, in turn, inform City and County Development Plans and contribute to Ireland's target of 80% renewables by 2030. Action EL/23/2 was due to be completed in 2023, however, publication of this Framework was delayed to ensure that these Regional Renewable Electricity targets could be merged with the revised National Planning Framework which was published in Q2. |  |  |

| Under this action, a Private Wires Policy Framework was developed, which contributes to the acceleration of renewable energy generation by allowing generators to connect directly with users, enabling quicker connections, more clean energy on the ground, and less strain on the national grid. It will also facilitate the provision of low-cost charging solutions for electric vehicles and expand opportunities for self-supply of electricity. Approved by Government in July 2025, the policy framework was informed by a 2023 public consultation with a diverse group of citizens, industry associations, system operators and private energy developers. It outlines four circumstances in which a private wire can be built: • A direct connectoi n between a generator, for example, a wind farm or solar farm, and an electricity customer, perhaps a factory that wants to decarbonise. There are situatoi ns where this is quicker and more efficient than having both partei s connect to the Grid • A wind farm or solar farm owned by one company will be able to share a grid connectoi n with another generator or a battery installatoi n owned by another firm. Previously, this could have breached the rules prohibitni g private lines; • Private lines will be allowed where they are the solutoi n to allow on-street charging of Electric Vehicles; and • To allow a firm that self-supplies electricity to provide electricity to neighbouring customers where the line does not have to cross land owned by a third party. |  |  |

### 8 Cross-cutting actions

*Pages 26–26*

Cross-cutting actions support the delivery of sectoral climate action through

improvements in governance, finance, planning, research, citizen engagement, circular

economy, adaptation, and public sector leadership. Several Departments and Agencies

contribute to the completion of such actions, providing a whole-of-Government

response and enabling framework for national climate action. Information on all cross-

cutting actions due for reporting is at Appendix 1.

Ireland is experiencing first-hand the growing impacts of climate change. Within the last

12 months, Storms Éowyn and Daragh brought power outages and extensive damage

to infrastructure. Investing in climate change adaptation measures now will help support

communities and businesses into the future.

In 2024, the Government approved the second National Adaptation Framework, which

outlines the national strategy to reduce the country’s vulnerability to the adverse effects

of climate change and to avail of positive impacts.

Earlier this year, the EPA published the first National Climate Change Risk Assessment,

which identified 115 risks, 43 of which were deemed significant, including flooding,

coastal erosion, heat, and extreme wind. It also highlighted five potential opportunities

for certain economic sectors in Ireland, such as a longer season for outdoor activities,

which encourages tourism, increased hydropower generation, greater marine species

diversity, longer growing season and improved livestock nutrition and grazing.

The CCAC's Annual Review on Preparing for Ireland’s Changing Climate emphasised the

urgency of implementing adaptation measures. It made key recommendations, including

ensuring permanent climate action roles in local authorities, establishing the Extreme

Weather Assistance Scheme before the 2025/26 storm season, and ensuring clearly

defined resilience outcomes in Sectoral Adaptation Plans. Progress has been made in

this area with ten Sectoral Adaptation Plans covering thirteen sectoral areas developed

in Q3, and Government approval is anticipated in Q4.
