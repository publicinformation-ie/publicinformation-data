### 1 Executive Summary

*Pages 3–6*

This Progress Report provides detail on the completion in 2026 of delayed or legacy

actions from previous iterations of the Climate Action Plan (CAP). In total, 10 of the 74

legacy actions were delivered by the end of Q2 2026 (Table 1). High impact actions

completed include: 2nd Circular Economy Strategy published; 200km of cycling

infrastructure completed; biofuel blend rates increased; Land Use Review Phase 2 report

published and a new National Demand Management Strategy for transport issued.

Completion of these legacy actions represents a significant milestone in Ireland’s climate

action journey, each being a meaningful high impact measure traced across from

previous Climate Action Plans. It is important to recognise their completion as these

actions experienced unexpected challenges and delays in delivery.

Overall implementation to date of CAP23, CAP24 and CAP25 legacy actions is outlined

in Table 2.

Notwithstanding this notable progress, delays in the delivery of remaining legacy actions

are attributed to several factors. This includes issues of alignment (waiting for

developments in other national policies, actions or EU legislation); capacity constraints

(internal resourcing and reliance on external consultants); technical complexity

(scale/type of project); consultation needs (stakeholders, public, internal etc) and other

discrete issues (e.g. funding and procurement). These delays threaten Ireland’s ability to

meet binding emissions reduction targets and risk increasing future climate changes,

compliance costs, and reputational harm. While much has been achieved and delivered

through successive CAPs, increased effort is required if Ireland is to close the gap to

meeting targets that it has agreed to. All delayed legacy actions will continue to be

reported on to maintain visibility on their delivery.

To provide more detail on progress, this report is organised into sectoral chapters in

order of emissions share, with a further chapter on cross-cutting activity. As in previous

progress reports, attention is given to Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) and the latest

emissions estimates and projections released by the EPA. Information on each reportable

action is contained at Appendix 1.

|  | Publication of a carbon farming framework |  |
| Did you know? Under CAP24, the Department of Agriculture Food and the Marine (DAFM) were due to publish a draft carbon farming framework by the end of Q2 2024. This action is now almost two years delayed, with its timeline originally repositioned in anticipation of the EU’s Carbon Removals and Carbon Farming (CRCF) Regulation. The EU framework published in December 2024. Why is it important? Carbon farming includes activity that leads to greater storage of carbon in living biomass, dead organic matter and soils by enhancing carbon capture and/or decreasing GHG emissions. It includes farm management activity that removes and stores carbon in soils and vegetation, such as planting hedgerows and trees, raising the water table on peaty grasslands, incorporating straw into soils and cover cropping. It may also include direct efforts to reduce emissions from farming such as adopting low emission technologies like protected urea and by improving efficiencies through better animal nutrition and genetics. The EU anticipates that carbon farming will remove several hundred million tonnes of CO2 per annum by 2030 which will be critical to achieving climate neutrality across Member States. A National Carbon Farming Framework would help to facilitate, recognise and ultimately reward farmers and land managers in Ireland for activity in this space and allow them to derive a new income stream that benefits climate action, biodiversity and water quality. Significant work has been undertaken to date in pursuit of this action. A public consultation on key principles for developing carbon farming in Ireland was held in late 2025. Incorporating its results, a National Carbon Farming Framework is in draft and with the DAFM expecting publication later this year. |  |  |

|  | Publication of National Demand Management Strategy |  |
| Did you know? Under CAP23, the Department of Transport were due to publish a National Demand Management Strategy for transport by the end of Q4 2024. The Strategy took a significant amount of additional work, analysis and stakeholder engagement to bring to completion and it was published in March 2026. Why is it important? Demand management is an essential pillar of emissions reductions in Transport in successive Climate Action Plans as part of the ‘Avoid-Shift-Improve’ framework. The newly published Demand Management Strategy, entitled ‘Moving Together: A Collaborative Approach to Systems Change in Transport 2026– 2030’, critically addresses the ‘Avoid’ piece of the established climate action framework. In essence, it involves eliminating the creation of unnecessary transport emissions by reducing travel demand through better planning and carefully designed schemes that create more efficiency and less congestion across the transport system. In turn, it is expected to bring benefits not only for reduced transport emissions but improved safety through fewer road collisions, reduced economic and social costs of congestion and improved air quality, health and wellbeing. The new Strategy aims to encourage collaboration across the public, private and community sectors and to find innovative ways for supporting this ambition. It will be implemented with four key principles in mind: Just Transition, Accessibility and Inclusion, Rural Recognition and Economic and Financial Sustainability. Rather than a prescriptive set of top-down actions, the Strategy lists 32 recommendations and is intended to provide the high-level direction required at a national level. It references a wide range of options that can be selected at national, regional and local level, aiming to guide, support and enable appropriate authorities in public and private domains to take ownership and shape their own actions in a coherent, evidence-based manner. |  |  |

|  | Publish a report on the work of the Carbon Capture, Utilisation and Storage Taskforce |  |
| Did you know? The Carbon Capture, Utilisation and Storage (CCUS) Taskforce was established to develop recommendations to Government on the potential deployment of CCUS in Ireland. A report was due from the group by the end of Q4 2025. Under the joint leadership of DCEE and DETE, a CCUS Vision Statement is being developed and is now expected to publish Q4 2026. Why is it important? CCUS can help to eliminate emissions from hard-to-abate sectors where decarbonisation is particularly challenging, such as cement, chemicals and other energy intensive industries. It is understood internationally that such technology will form a key and necessary component of net-zero ambitions by 2050, with a need now to understand how it could be used in an Irish context to meet legally binding targets and help avoid further costs of compliance. By capturing carbon dioxide before it enters the atmosphere and either storing it or using it in industrial processes, CCUS can support Ireland’s transition to net zero and help to protect industrial competitiveness. Projects are already developing in Norway, Denmark and the Netherlands further demonstrating its potential, including the development of offshore carbon stores with a mix of ship and pipeline carbon transport solutions. Feasibility and validation of CCUS in Ireland is essential to unlock those opportunities and could involve retrofitting existing cement, power plants, and waste-to-energy facilities with carbon capture capabilities, as well as building necessary compression, transport and storage infrastructure (e.g., creating dedicated port areas for international shipment of carbon for industrial use or geological storage elsewhere). |  |  |

| Did you know? Under CAP25, the Department of Climate Energy and the Environment, in collaboration with other Departments, was due to publish Phase 2 of the Land Use Review by Q2 2025. While this complex legacy action experienced some delays, the report from Phase 2 was approved by Government in Q2 2026 and subsequently published online here. Why is it important? Publication of Phase 2 of the Land Use Review marks a significant milestone in consolidating evidence that will help to determine optimal use of land across Ireland in ways that improve socioeconomic, climate, biodiversity, water, and air quality outcomes. While not an adopted policy or strategy, 'A Living Land' provides an important evidence base for Government, supporting policymakers and landholders in decision making regarding competing demands for future land-use, including in the context of developing more resilience to climate change and achieving a climate neutral economy by 2050. The report notes the importance of continued government leadership in leveraging state-owned lands to achieve integrated land use objectives, including for afforestation and peatland restoration. It also acknowledges however that c.80% of Irish land is in private ownership, and therefore decisions regarding potential uses is a matter for custodians of the land. Creative and inspiring community and farm-level projects are included in the report providing real world case studies that can be scaled to better deliver on environmental and socioeconomic objectives from land, including the investment in locally led agri-environmental schemes. Further geospatial mapping and data synthesis was also undertaken as part of Phase 2 to improve and refine decision making in this area. To take key recommendations forward, a new governance structure will be established in the form of a Land Use Taskforce, to be co-chaired by DCEE and DAFM at Assistant Secretary level. |  |  |

| Did you know? The second Whole of Government Circular Economy Strategy 2026- 2028 was published in February 2026, completing an important outstanding CAP23 action. The Strategy positions circularity as central to Ireland’s economic competitiveness, environmental sustainability, and social wellbeing, moving Ireland from a linear “take–make–waste” model to a regenerative, closed-loop system where materials are kept in use for as long as possible. It represents the next phase in Ireland’s transition towards a resource-efficient, innovation-driven, and climate neutral economy and builds upon the first Circular Economy Strategy (2021–2023) published in 2021. The new Strategy sets actions and targets across key priority sectors that will deliver environmental, social and economic benefits. Why is it important? The second Circular Economy Strategy is important because it provides a framework for reducing Ireland’s consumption of raw materials and minimising waste generation, and therefore lowering greenhouse gas emissions associated with both endeavours. It also presents many co-benefits. For example, the Strategy supports economic growth and business opportunities in a more sustainable way by promoting the reuse, repair, refurbishment and recycling of products and materials. The Strategy also works to reduce dependence on imported resources which will help to strengthen supply chain security and strengthen State and business resilience and competitiveness in the face of supply shocks, all the while creating new employment opportunities. Building out a circular economy supports Ireland’s climate and environmental objectives by helping to reduce resource extraction and waste, while making more efficient use of resources. |  |  |

### 5 Buildings

*Pages 15–15*

residential). Ireland is progressing a major transformation of its building stock, including

ambitions to retrofit 500,000 homes by 2030, roll out large-scale district heating, and

enhance the energy performance of commercial buildings. In 2025, residential emissions

decreased by 5% since 2024, underpinned by a milder winter resulting in less heating

degree days. Residential use of peat remained unchanged, but the consumption of coal,

oil and natural gas decreased by 13.5%, 4.8% and 5.2% respectively. This reduced

emissions per household to 2.7.t CO2eq, representing an all-time low since 1990.

### 6 Industry

*Pages 18–18*

In 2025, industrial emissions accounted for 11% of Ireland's total emissions.

Manufacturing combustion and industrial processes are the key emission sources in this

sector. Industry emissions are largely affected by cement production in Ireland, which in

turn are impacted by economic cycles. Manufacturing combustion emissions decreased

by 3.9% in 2025 due to declines in coal, oil and natural gas use in industry and renewable

fuel use increasing by 7.9%. Total emissions from the cement sector decreased by 3.6%

in 2025, though remain 44.6% higher than in 2011 during the economic recession.

### 8 Cross-cutting actions

*Pages 24–24*

Cross-cutting actions support the delivery of sectoral climate action through

improvements in governance, finance, planning, research, citizen engagement, circular

economy, adaptation, and public sector leadership. Several Departments and Agencies

contribute to the completion of such actions, providing a whole-of-Government

response and enabling framework for national climate action. Information on all cross-

cutting actions due for reporting is at Appendix 1.

In total, 2 of the 25 remaining cross-cutting legacy actions were completed this quarter.

These include the publication of Ireland's second Whole of Government Circular

Economy Strategy (see case study), and the publication of the Decarbonising Zones

(DZs) Advisory Group annual report outlining progress, and opportunities and challenges

faced by Local Authorities in implementing such activity.

Key legacy actions that remain delayed across the system include:

•the publication of the Public Sector’s 2024 and 2025 building decarbonisation

roadmaps;

•the introduction of levies on single-use packaging for disposable cups for cold

drinks;

•the development of a Circular Economy Roadmap for the Construction Sector;

•explorations of national supplies of critical raw materials;

•policy support tools to embed Just Transition principles;

•the completion of climate action roadmaps by all public bodies; and

•legislation for the management of Marine Protected Areas in Irish marine waters.

Delayed legacy actions span topics and areas that impact across the six high impact

emissions sectors detailed above. They form an essential part of the supports and

complimentary actions that will enable progress towards national and EU climate targets.
